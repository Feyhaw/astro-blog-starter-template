Commonly refered to as the "DEW Line." It is a chain of radar stations from Alaska to Greenland, built to monitor the air space over the Arctic. In case of Soviet Union attack by bombers, it will send out warning, buying 3 to 6 hours for Canada and United States to prepare. 

<strong>project initiation</strong>

The earliest proposal can be traced back to Air Force’s “Supremacy” plan in 1946: a chain of radar stations from Alaska across Canada to Greenland. [1](#ref-1) The planning began in 1949 following the detonation of Soviet Union’s nuclear bomb, the USAF commissioned MIT to study the existing air defence structure against nuclear threats, resulting as “Project Charles.”[2](#ref-2) The prototype of SAGE air defence system was sucessfully tested in September 1951, proving the project's viability.[3](#ref-3) It soon evolved into “Project Lincoln” with roughly the same study crew. In August 1952, Lincoln Laboratory summer study proposed to build a line of radar station by 70th parallel connecting Alaska and Northeast Command of Canada.[4](#ref-4) In the beginning, the proposed plan for continental defence did not meet positive treatment from the public, but was endorsed by [Vannevar Bush](/dictionary/vannevarbush) and [Lloyd Berkner](/dictionary/lloydberkner). In 1953, The newly elected President Eisenhower, who deemed air defence system necessary to deter wars with massive retaliation.[5](#ref-5) After the successful test at Barter Island and Illinois facility, the program officially commenced in December 1954, with a deadline set on July 31, 1957. In 1956, the system was extended to Greenland ("DYE" stations).

<strong>System and architecture</strong>

The task of designing and building the stations were given to Western Electric Company; the radars were built by Motorola and [Raytheon](/dictionary/raytheon).[6](#ref-6) Previous geographical surveys conducted by [AINA](/dictionary/aina) were supplied for the construction.[7](#ref-7) Stationes were divided into 3 categories: main (in total of 6), auxiliary (in total of 23), intermediate (in total of 28).[8](#ref-8) The stations employed modular construction: 2 trains of 25 modules at main stations, 1 train of 25 modules at auxiliary stations, and 5 at intermediate stations. Modules were prefabricated into functions including sleeping, office, dining, recreation, and storage. In addition, different types of radar were installed; only main and auxiliary stations were equiped with FPS-19 long-range radar.[9](#ref-9) To shelter FPS-19 radars, MIT staff Jerome Wiesner approached [Richard Buckminster Fuller](/dictionary/bucky) to negotiate a design of geodesic dome; the dome's success led to military's reward of a patent to Buckminster Fuller.[10](#ref-10) DEW Line's computer system was based on MIT's [Whirlwind computer], and the stations were equiped with an experiemntal automatic alert system to reflief human from constant monitoring work.[11](#ref-11)

<strong>Later development</strong>

The radar and the automatic altert system were proven to be unreliable in the early day's of DEW Line operation.[12](#ref-12) Three months after the completion of DEW Line, it was already rendered obsolete by the launch of [Sputnik], which shed the first light of the inter-continental ballistic missile era. The planns to add new device to detect missiles were scrapped by the US Air Force in 1960 due to fun shortage.[13](#ref-13) In July 1963, The USAF ordered inactivation of all 28 intermediate sites (20 in Canada and 8 in Alaska); by July 1, 1963, 57 stations were reduced to 28. [14](#ref-14) In 1993, 15 DEW Line stations were converted to the umnanned North Warning System, and the rest were decommissioned.[15](#ref-15) Despite the technological failures in DEW Line's hasty deployment, the system is the world's first air-traffic monitoring system. It introduced the earliest human-machine interface and paved the way for Apollo program. Moreover, it forced Canada to integrate its vast Arctic territory into the national political, economic, and welfare systems.
