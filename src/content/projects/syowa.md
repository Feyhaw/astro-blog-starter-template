title: Metabolising in the Ice
subtitle: From Syowa Station to Metabolist capsules

description: Historical and architectural research of Japan Antarctic Syowa Station and its ties with the Metabolism Movement

abstract: | 
    Are Antarctic stations the most “metabolising” structures? Four years into the demolition of Nakagin Capsule Tower, the Metabolism movement seems a distant memory of an age when ambitious architects still sought to guide social progress, with the government as a powerful ally and spontaneous growth of city as a technological solution rather than a biological metaphor. While the “main stem” of Metabolism seemed to have ceased to renew in Japan, Syowa Station (Syowa Kichi),  designed by almost the same people, slowly expanded into a small town over the decades. This section will focus on Syowa Station, including its design, history, and ties to contemporary architecture, especially Metabolism. Within the broader theme of environmental control, this section will explore control technologies and arrangements through the lens of capsule architecture.
    
    This research on the architecture of Syowa Station introduces the Japanese polar architecture that is little known to the Western architectural community. More importantly, it addresses an overlooked, perhaps extreme, case of Metabolism architecture: one pushed to the limit, where capsules are no longer an option but a necessity. Original Japanese archive materials are consulted to facilitate the research: specifically, writings on Metabolism and Syowa Station are cross-referenced. Visual materials such as drawings and photos are analysed and compared. Although Metabolism and polar architecture have been made known to the public, the missing link between the two provides new understandings of both. In addition to the hidden connection between the two, I also find a corresponding timeline between Syowa Station and Metabolism architecture; both of which can be divided into three generations. Research on Syowa Station not only deepens understanding of past movement and the society that gave birth to it but also inspires new ideas for future polar habitats.
